## 1. Part 1 — Constructivist epistemologies

### 1.1 What the session addresses

The programme frames the session around a shift in the foundations of knowledge: instead of assuming that a phenomenon can be known as it exists independently of the observer, constructivist epistemologies emphasize that knowledge is developed through an observer's experience and modelling activity. The programme explicitly avoids requiring a prior metaphysical answer about whether an observer-independent reality exists; it stresses that any knowledge claim is mediated by the process through which the phenomenon is perceived and represented (R-166, R-167).

The session's participants bring different professional and intellectual contexts. The organizer's introduction by Georges Garcia asks how constructivist epistemologies change the way we question phenomena. A related contribution by Dominique Genelot cautions that “complexity” is used in very different ways depending on epistemological commitments. Jean-Yves Rossignol's later 2024 testimony describes a pragmatic-constructivist application in engineering pedagogy; it is useful contextual evidence, but it postdates the 2023 session and must not be retroactively attributed to the panel (R-170).

### 1.2 Core ideas worth examining

- **Knowledge is constructed, not simply copied.** A model is an intellectual and cultural construction shaped by a question, purpose, language, instruments, observations and prior concepts. It is not a transparent mirror of the world.
- **The observer is part of the epistemic situation.** The modeller's objectives, selected boundaries, relevant variables and preferred explanations affect what is made visible. This does not mean that the modeller can make any claim true by choosing it.
- **Validity requires explicit criteria.** The important question is not merely “is this model true?” but “for which purpose, under which assumptions, against which observations, according to which criteria, and within which limits is this representation adequate?”
- **Constructivism is not one doctrine.** Radical constructivism, social constructivism, pragmatic constructivism and constructivist approaches in organizational research are not interchangeable. Avenier's pragmatic constructivist work emphasizes the disciplined production and legitimization of knowledge, including knowledge built from practitioners' experience (R-171).
- **Epistemic reflexivity is a discipline.** Researchers and engineers must make visible how they produced knowledge, what they excluded, how they challenged it and how others can criticize or reproduce the reasoning.

### 1.3 Critical balance

The colloquium's framing is compatible with a modest and useful thesis: access to a phenomenon is mediated by observation and modelling, and models are purpose-relative. It should not be simplified into “there is no reality”, “all models are equally valid” or “truth is only consensus”.

There are substantive disagreements in the literature. Critics of radical constructivism have argued that some formulations risk anti-realism, relativism or individualism (R-172). Other work argues that constructivist insights can be retained without abandoning a robust account of truth (R-173). A critical review of Le Moigne's own argumentative construction raises questions about his use of conceptual genealogies and argumentative transitions (R-174). These critiques are not interchangeable and do not refute every constructivist approach; they show why GovCheck should not adopt “constructivism” as an undifferentiated label.

A pragmatic approach can instead require explicit purposes, declared assumptions, public criteria, empirical confrontation, criticism, independent evidence and traceable revisions. This permits situated knowledge without collapsing into arbitrary relativism.

### 1.4 Possible relevance to GovCheck

This session raises foundational questions for GovCheck's own epistemology:

- A governance model is a representation of a repository, organization, workflow and assurance context; it is not the repository or organization itself.
- Requirements, intentions, risks, models and assurance claims are formulated from viewpoints and purposes. Their scope and applicability must be stated.
- A claim's validity must be assessed relative to its declared context, assumptions, evidence and acceptance criteria.
- The person or authority who defines the model, selects evidence and accepts residual risk is part of the governance situation and must be visible.
- Multiple models may be needed for different concerns (e.g. lifecycle, access control, assurance, threat, semantic interoperability). One universal model may conceal rather than resolve important differences.

**Research question, not conclusion:** can GovCheck express observer, viewpoint, purpose, model boundary, assumptions, evidence and validity context using existing concepts? If not, is the gap genuinely ontological, or can it be addressed by more precise semantics and traceability?

## 2. Part 2 — Assuming and going beyond the irreducible

### 2.1 What the session addresses

The programme describes a difficulty encountered when a situation contains so many interrelated events, processes and meanings that it cannot be reduced to one model, however complicated. The question is whether this should be treated as an impossibility of modelling or whether a different way of modelling can work with what remains irreducible (R-166, R-168).

Robert Delorme's published work offers an important companion to the session. His account of “second-order complexity” treats irreducibility as both an obstacle and a resource for knowledge and action. It distinguishes several forms: deterministic unpredictability, radical probabilistic/statistical uncertainty, indecomposability, algorithmic incompressibility and lack of controllability. It argues that no one form is automatically the most relevant, and that object-level and meta-level reasoning must be considered together (R-175).

### 2.2 Core ideas worth examining

- **Complexity is not just a large number of parts.** A situation may resist decomposition because relations, feedback, history, purpose or emergent effects are essential to its identity.
- **Irreducibility is relative to a modelling purpose and reduction criterion.** “Cannot be reduced” needs an explicit account: irreducible for which question, at what resolution, under what constraints and with which acceptable loss?
- **Different kinds of uncertainty must not be conflated.** Incomplete knowledge, stochastic uncertainty, deterministic unpredictability, computational intractability and uncontrollability require different treatment.
- **Object and meta levels interact.** A model can describe a system while a second-order model examines how the first model was constructed, what it excludes and how its adequacy is judged.
- **Abduction and local theorizing matter.** Delorme's work highlights the construction of explanatory hypotheses and context-specific models rather than forcing every case into a single universal reduction.
- **“Going beyond” does not mean eliminating irreducibility.** It can mean constructing useful, explicit, revisable representations while retaining the parts that remain uncertain, unmodelled or uncontrollable.

### 2.3 Critical balance

Delorme's companion text is conceptually rich, but terms such as “irreducibility”, “second order” and “complexity” need operational definitions before they can support a machine-checkable governance rule. A project must say what it cannot currently explain or control, what evidence supports that assessment, what modelling loss is accepted and who accepts the risk.

A second-order account can itself become an unbounded regress: who validates the model of the model, and how far must the observer's assumptions be modelled? A practical stopping rule is needed, grounded in purpose, decision consequences, available evidence and accepted uncertainty.

The five forms of complexity listed in Delorme's paper are not a universal taxonomy. They are a research framework to test against the specific domain, not a set of mandatory GovCheck classes. A proof assistant or model checker can establish results about a formalized abstraction; it cannot, by that fact alone, show that the abstraction captures every relevant feature of a complex socio-technical situation.

### 2.4 Possible relevance to GovCheck

This session has a strong conceptual connection to GovCheck's treatment of assurance gaps and residual risk:

- Avoid implying that a complete-looking governance model exhausts the repository's real operating context.
- Record known unknowns, model limitations, unmodelled interactions and external dependencies.
- Distinguish uncertainty, missing evidence, computational limits, uncontrollability and deliberate scope exclusion.
- Permit several partial models and assurance arguments to coexist, each with explicit scope and assumptions.
- Require a responsible authority to accept residual risk rather than silently treating an absent model or evidence item as harmless.
- Keep meta-level critique available: a claim about model adequacy should itself identify its criteria and evidence.

**Research question, not conclusion:** can the existing GovCheck assurance model represent irreducibility as a documented limitation and decision-relevant uncertainty, without turning “complexity” into a vague risk label or a new mandatory taxonomy?

## 3. Part 3 — Systemic modelling

### 3.1 What the session addresses

The official programme describes a round table on what distinguishes systemic modelling from classical Cartesian/analytic modelling. It identifies Le Moigne's “General System” as a conceptual instrument based on constructivist epistemologies and intended to support the intelligibility and organization of complex phenomena. The programme refers to five properties but does not enumerate them in the replay summary (R-166, R-169).

Le Moigne's *La théorie du système général : théorie de la modélisation* is explicit that the central concern is not only the finished model but the process of designing a model in a concrete situation. Its organization around an active, structured and evolving system links purpose, action, environment, organization, regulation and evolution. A contemporary bibliographic account and an early scholarly review help clarify the distinction between a “theory of the General System” and a generic “general systems theory” (R-176, R-177).

### 3.2 Core ideas worth examining

- **Model-making is an activity.** The modelling process, its actors, purposes, languages, choices and revisions are part of the object of inquiry.
- **Purpose and action matter.** A system is not described only by its components and static structure; the model considers what it does, what it is intended to do and how it changes its environment.
- **Structure, function and evolution are complementary views.** The early review of Le Moigne describes functional/action, structural and genetic/evolutionary dimensions; a model that captures only one dimension may miss essential behavior (R-177).
- **Environment is relational.** A system boundary is a modelling decision; dependencies and exchanges across the boundary may determine whether the model is adequate.
- **Organization is more than component inventory.** Relations, coordination, regulation, memory and adaptation are part of systemic intelligibility.
- **A model is a communicable construction.** The modelling language and its semantics must be explicit enough that different participants can understand, criticize and use the representation.

### 3.3 Critical balance

Systemic modelling is not a licence to avoid precision. Its value depends on making the model's purpose, concepts, boundaries, transformations and criteria explicit. A broad systems diagram without operational semantics can become less testable than a narrow formal model.

Nor should the “General System” be imported as a ready-made ontology for GovCheck. Its concepts were developed for a particular theoretical project. The proper task is comparative: identify which concepts illuminate GovCheck's existing lifecycle, authority, context, environment and assurance representations; identify overlaps and incompatibilities; then determine whether any genuine gap remains.

Soft Systems Methodology provides a useful adjacent comparison. Checkland's approach uses multiple purposeful conceptual models to support debate and learning in situations with obscure objectives and legitimate competing viewpoints. In SSM, models are not simply predictive replicas of reality; they are devices for structured discussion and improvement (R-178, R-179). Model-based systems engineering provides another counterpoint: models can support design, analysis, verification and validation, but their validity context and limits must be maintained throughout their lifecycle (R-180, R-181, R-182).

### 3.4 Possible relevance to GovCheck

Potentially useful questions include:

- Does the governance model describe only static artefacts, or also the activities and transformations through which they are created, challenged, accepted and revised?
- Are purpose, intended outcomes, operating environment, dependencies and system boundaries explicit?
- Can a model express feedback, adaptation, evolution and changing assumptions without conflating them with immutable identity?
- Can different stakeholders maintain distinct but related views without falsely claiming that one view exhausts the system?
- Can formal models be linked to empirical observations and operational evidence while keeping the abstraction boundary explicit?
- Can GovCheck record model validity context and the conditions under which a model must be reconsidered?

**Research question, not conclusion:** should systemic modelling influence the way GovCheck represents the lifecycle and context of governance, or is its contribution already covered by existing concepts and linked models?

## 4. Synthesis across the three sessions

The three sessions form a coherent progression:

1. **Epistemology:** how is knowledge constructed, and under what conditions can it be considered valid?
2. **Irreducibility:** what happens when the phenomenon cannot be adequately reduced to one model, and how should uncertainty and limits be handled?
3. **Systemic modelling:** how can a purposeful, evolving and interconnected situation be represented without pretending the model is the reality?

Together, they suggest a disciplined stance: models are purpose-relative constructions; they must expose their assumptions, boundaries and validity contexts; complexity may leave irreducible uncertainty; and useful modelling should support both understanding and responsible action.

This is compatible with formalization, but it places a crucial limit on what formal verification means. A theorem prover checks a proposition under formal definitions and assumptions. A model checker explores a defined transition system. Neither result, by itself, establishes that the selected model is an adequate representation of the socio-technical phenomenon, that the assumptions hold in operation, or that the chosen purpose is legitimate. Those are separate assurance questions requiring argument, evidence, observation and authorized judgment.

## 5. Research implications for GovCheck — deliberately non-normative

The strongest candidate contribution is not a new “constructivist” or “systemic” layer in the metamodel. It is a set of questions to test against the existing architecture and specifications:

| Research concern | Question for the existing GovCheck model | What must not be inferred |
|---|---|---|
| Observer and viewpoint | Can authorship, viewpoint, purpose and authority behind a model/claim be traced? | That truth is merely subjective or decided by consensus |
| Model boundary | Can scope, environment, exclusions and dependencies be explicit? | That a declared boundary captures every relevant dependency |
| Validity context | Can a model state assumptions, criteria, evidence, intended use and limits? | That formal consistency establishes real-world adequacy |
| Irreducibility | Can unknowns, uncertainty, unmodelled interactions and uncontrollability be distinguished? | That all uncertainty is one scalar risk or one complexity class |
| Plural models | Can partial, purpose-specific models coexist and be compared? | That incompatible models are automatically equally valid |
| Evolution | Can models and assumptions be revised with provenance and impact analysis? | That a new revision automatically supersedes all prior evidence |
| Formal assurance | Can proof/model-checking results be scoped to exact abstractions and assumptions? | That a checked model proves the implementation or the world |

### 5.1 What GovCheck may eventually tell users

This research may eventually support a user-facing explanation that GovCheck is particularly relevant where a project needs explicit, traceable governance of models, decisions, assumptions, evidence and change over time. It may be useful in complex or multi-stakeholder settings precisely because it can make boundaries and disagreements visible.

It must not claim to solve complexity, provide a complete representation of reality, or replace domain expertise, participatory inquiry, empirical observation or accountable risk ownership. A project whose central need is rich stakeholder facilitation or continuous operational systems modelling may need complementary methods and tools. That does not automatically make GovCheck unsuitable; it means its contribution and prerequisites must be described honestly.

No binary “complexity score” or universal maturity threshold is supported by these sources.
